Speed to Lead 2026

Speed to lead: what missed calls cost contractors, and how to put a number on it.

You pay for the ring through ads, reviews, and your Google profile. Then the phone goes to voicemail. Here is what the data says, how to calculate your own loss, and the fixes that cost little.

BY NICK CONLEY FOUNDER · BLUE COLLAR TECHY
// The leak you are not measuring

You can spend a few thousand dollars a month on ads, Google Business Profile work, and yard signs, and lose a chunk of it at the last step. The phone rings. Nobody answers. The caller dials the next contractor on the list.

Think of it like laying pavers on a bad base. The finished surface looks great for a season. Then it shifts, because the part nobody sees was never built right. Speed to lead is the base. It means how fast a new customer hears back from you after they call, text, or fill out a form. If you skip it, the money you spent on the surface goes to waste.

This post covers what the research says about speed to lead for contractors, how to put a dollar figure on your own missed calls, and the fixes that cost little or nothing, including a missed call text back. I also flag where the data is thin, because some of the stats floating around are shakier than they look.

I learned this the hard way. When I was first starting out, I can't remember how many calls I missed. I'd call people back days later, only to be told, "Oh, we found somebody else." I lost tens of thousands of dollars' worth of work by not answering my phone. That's why one of my first hires was an office person to answer it. Once I did that, it seemed like on every single call, people were blown away that we answered. That's why we got most of our jobs. We just answered the phone, and they didn't have to leave a voicemail.

// What the data says

How many calls go unanswered, and what callers do next.

Start with the best available data, and its limits.

  • 27% of calls to home services businesses go unanswered. That figure comes from call-tracking company Invoca, in a post published May 23, 2024 and drawn from calls on its platform. Invoca sells call-tracking software, so it has a stake in the answer, and it writes for large multi-location companies. A one-truck shop could be better or worse.
  • Under 3% of callers sent to voicemail leave a message. Same source, same caveat. Treat it as a rough guide: most people who hit voicemail hang up and keep looking.
  • 62% of home services consumers said they would call before making a purchase. From an Invoca survey in the same post. Research happens online. The decision often happens on the phone.

For web forms and quote requests, the best-known research is a Harvard Business Review article, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads," published in March 2011. It describes two studies.

  • An audit of 2,241 US companies. Researchers sent each one a web-generated test lead and timed the reply. 37% responded within an hour, 24% took more than 24 hours, and 23% never responded at all. Among companies that did reply within 30 days, the average response time was 42 hours.
  • A study of 1.25 million sales leads at 42 US companies, 29 selling to consumers and 13 selling to businesses. Companies that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it as companies that tried even an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as companies that waited 24 hours or longer.

Three limits before you lean on those numbers. The data was published in 2011, so it is more than 15 years old. The companies were in fields like financial services, autos, education, software, and health care, not contractors. And "qualify" meant having a meaningful conversation with a decision maker, which is a step short of a signed job. You will also see a "21 times more likely within five minutes" claim online. The HBR article's fastest window is one hour, so that number comes from somewhere else, and I do not use it.

So what can I say honestly about speed to lead? Fast response is associated with more real conversations, callers who hit voicemail rarely leave a message, and nobody has published a clean number for your trade in your town. Your own phone records can fill that gap, and the next section shows how.

// Your number

Put a dollar figure on your own missed calls.

You need three numbers. Pull them from your phone's call log for the last 30 days, or from your call tracking if you have it.

  1. Missed calls from new customers. Unanswered calls from numbers you do not recognize. Ignore calls from vendors and existing clients.
  2. Your close rate on new inquiries. Of the people you do talk to, what share end up signing? If you do not know, use your last 20 inquiries.
  3. Your average job profit. Use profit after materials and labor. Revenue makes the number look bigger than it is.

Then run the math:

Missed new-customer calls × your close rate × average job profit = profit left on the table per month

Here is a made-up example with round numbers, so you can follow the math. It is an illustration only and should not be read as a benchmark. Say you miss 20 new-customer calls in a month. You close one of every four inquiries, so 25%. An average job nets you $2,500 in profit. That is 20 × 0.25 × $2,500 = $12,500.

That example assumes every missed caller would have behaved like a caller you reached. They would not. Cut the number in half if it makes you more comfortable. Then compare it to what you spend on marketing in a month.

You paid for the ring. Answer it.
The cheapest fix in marketing
// Fix 1

Answer more of the calls you are already paying for.

Before you buy anything, tighten what you have.

  • Decide who answers, every day. If it is "whoever is closest to the phone," nobody owns it.
  • Make your Google Business Profile hours match reality. If you are listed open and you never pick up at 7 a.m., you are advertising a service you do not provide.
  • Return calls in batches, on a schedule. Blocks at 10, 12, and 3 beat "when I get a minute."
  • Ring more than one phone. Forward the line to a second person when you are on a roof.
  • Cover the hours you cannot. If nobody can pick up during busy stretches, a live answering service can take a message and book the callback. Get a quote and compare it to the dollar figure you worked out above.
  • If you run paid ads, check when calls come in. If most of your missed calls are during the day, you may be paying for clicks while the whole crew is on a job.
// Fix 2

Missed call text back: send a text within a minute.

You cannot always pick up. You can always reply. A missed call text back sends an automatic text to a caller you did not answer, so they hear from you before they dial the next name on the list. Many contractor CRMs include this, GoHighLevel among them. Check what yours supports, and look at the cost before you commit.

Keep it short and human. Say who you are, say you saw the call, and ask one easy question.

Missed-call text, example
Hi, this is [your name] with [company]. Sorry I missed your call, we are on a job site. What can I help you with? I can call you back at [time window] or you can reply here. Reply STOP to opt out.

Two cautions. First, texting rules are real. In the US, the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) and the FCC's rules cover automated texts. The FCC says autodialed texts to a cell phone need the owner's prior consent, that commercial texts need written consent, and that people can opt out of any robotext at any time in any reasonable way. Replying to someone who just called you is a different situation from cold texting, but I am not a lawyer. Ask yours, or ask your CRM provider how they handle consent and opt-outs like STOP. Second, do not let the automation pretend to be a person who is standing by. If you sent an auto-reply, follow up with a real call.

// Fix 3

Treat web forms like phone calls, and call back with a plan.

A form submission is a call that came in writing. Set a target and measure it. I use five minutes during business hours as a goal. That is my own target, and no study backs the exact number.

  1. Get form alerts to your phone. Use a text or push notification. An email you check at night is too slow.
  2. Reply with a real question. "What are you hoping to build, and when are you hoping to start?" gets answers. "Thanks for contacting us" does not.
  3. Call after the text. Many people prefer a quick call. Offer both.
  4. Follow up three times over a week. A short, polite second and third message can win jobs the first one missed.

When you do call back, open with the reason you are calling and one question. A callback that starts with "you called me?" wastes the one advantage you have, which is speed.

  • Lead with their request. "You called about a patio. Tell me what you have in mind."
  • Ask about timing and budget early. It saves both of you a wasted site visit.
  • Book the next step before you hang up. A site visit on the calendar beats "I will send you something."
  • Send a text right after the call confirming the time and address.

If your leads come from several places, such as Google, a directory, and your website, check how fast each one reaches you. A lead that lands in a spam folder is a missed call too.

Written inquiries are one place software can help. My agency, Tekton Growth, builds Mason, an AI assistant that drafts customer email replies for your review. Whatever you use, a person should check the reply before it goes out.

// This week

What to do in the next seven days.

Pull your call log for the last 30 days and count the missed new-customer calls. Do the math in the formula above. Pick one fix, whether that is an owner for the phone, an automatic text-back, or a faster form reply, and finish it before you touch your marketing.

Then count it every week, or it will drift back. Pick a day, Friday works, and write down four numbers: calls received from new customers, calls missed, texts sent back within a few minutes, and jobs booked from the week. It takes five minutes. Within a month you will see a pattern. Maybe it is Monday mornings, the lunch hour, or one lead source. You cannot fix a pattern you have not written down.

If you work with a marketing company, ask them for the same four numbers. A good one will have them. If they only report rankings and clicks, ask why. Booked jobs are what pay your crew.

If you want to see where your Google presence stands in your city, run the free GBP Health Check. It takes a few minutes and shows what a homeowner sees before they call. And if you are not sure whether a slow stretch is a market problem, a marketing problem, or a you problem, read when leads dry up: is it the market, the agency, or you. Missed calls belong in that conversation too.

The Mindset Shift

The small jobs are marketing. Most contractors don't see it that way.

Niche is a luxury when the market supports it. When the market shifts, the leads outside your usual lane stop being a distraction and start being some of the cheapest marketing money you'll ever spend.

BY NICK CONLEY FOUNDER · BLUE COLLAR TECHY
// The setup

Markets have seasons.

The same trade business that ran flat-out for five years can hit a stretch where the phone just goes quieter. Different leads start coming in. Smaller jobs. Adjacent services. Stuff that doesn't fit the version of the business you spent years building.

The first instinct most owners have is to filter those leads out. They don't fit the niche. They aren't the work you want. So you say no, and you wait for the work you do want.

I've watched a lot of trade businesses run that play. Sometimes the market is forgiving and the perfect leads come back. Sometimes they don't. And in the middle of that wait, a piece of marketing that costs almost nothing is sitting in your inbox getting ignored.

This post is about that piece. It's a mindset shift more than a tactic. If you read it and walk away thinking differently about the next "wrong-shaped" lead that comes through your door, that's the win.

// 01 · Niche is a real strategy. So is staying nimble.

Niche is a real strategy. So is staying nimble.

Let's get one thing out of the way up front.

There are markets where being narrowly niche works. There are zip codes where a contractor can spend their entire career installing only one thing and never lack for work. Some trades have so much demand stacked into so dense an area that the market does the filtering for you.

If that's your market, this post isn't telling you to abandon what's working. Niche is a strategy. It's how a lot of strong businesses get built.

But niche is also a market condition as much as a business decision. You're niche because the demand around you supports it. The day the demand thins, you're either still niche by choice, or you're niche by stubbornness. There's a real difference between the two, and most owners can't tell which one they're in until it's been a problem for a while.

Being nimble is a different kind of strategy. It says "I read what the market is doing this season and I match my mix to it." Some seasons that's pure niche work. Some seasons that's a wider lane. The contractors I've seen weather the most cycles aren't the ones with the cleanest niche. They're the ones who can read the room and adjust.

You don't have to abandon niche to be nimble. You just have to know which season you're in.

// 02 · A lead outside your niche isn't a bad lead. It's a marketing dollar in disguise.

A lead outside your niche isn't a bad lead. It's a marketing dollar in disguise.

Here's the reframe that I think matters most.

Marketing, in the way most contractors think about it, is the stuff with line items. SEO. Google Ads. LSAs. Facebook. Print, mailers, truck wraps. You write a check, you get visibility, hopefully you get a lead, hopefully you close it.

That definition misses the cheapest marketing dollar in the trades, which is the relationship you build with a homeowner who's already paid you for something.

Every existing customer is more valuable than every new one. Every conversation you have with a homeowner standing in their backyard is worth more than any ad impression you'll ever buy. The "yeah, I used those guys, they were great" line they drop at a kid's soccer game on Saturday is worth more than a month of LSA spend.

When a lead comes in that's outside your niche, the cost of capture is already covered. They found you. They called you. They're ready to pay somebody. The only question is whether you say yes to a relationship for the price of a half-day job, or whether you let your competitor build that relationship instead.

Saying no to that lead isn't keeping your business clean. It's writing your competitor a marketing check.

// 03 · The relationship math

Run the relationship math.

Run actual numbers in your head.

The average homeowner is a customer for forty or fifty years. They own a house. They renovate. They expand. They re-do the patio. They put in a pool. They re-side. They re-roof. Their kids grow up and they need a yard for graduation parties. Their parents move in and they need an accessible bathroom. Life happens, and homes get worked on every step of the way.

Every contractor who does a job for that homeowner becomes a candidate for the next job. Some of those contractors get the call back. Some don't. The ones who do are the ones the homeowner remembers liking. Not the ones with the prettiest portfolio. The ones who showed up, did the work, treated them well, and didn't make them feel small for the size of the job.

A two-thousand-dollar half-day job is one entry point into that forty-year relationship. You're not making your money on the half-day job. You're making your money on the patio they call you for the next spring. On the retaining wall the year after. On the outdoor kitchen four years from now. On their neighbor across the fence who watched the whole thing go down and asked who you were.

The "small job" is the cheapest customer acquisition cost in the trades, and most contractors are turning it down.

Saying no to a small job isn't keeping your business clean. It's writing your competitor a marketing check.
The reframe
// 04 · This is what I learned running my own hardscape business

This is what I learned running my own hardscape business.

I ran a hardscape company in Northern California for ten years. We hit three million in revenue at the peak. By any number on a tax return, we were the success story.

We worked in a market with real money in it. Beautiful properties, big budgets, the kind of clientele that hires real installers. The problem was where most of that budget went. Homeowners would remodel the kitchen, redo the bathrooms, finish the basement, and then come outside with whatever was left. By the time they got to us, the dream patio had become a smaller patio. The outdoor kitchen had become a grill on a slab. The retaining wall got value-engineered into railroad ties.

I started realizing the conversation I needed to be in was the one happening before the kitchen decision, not the one happening after. That's why we launched Tekton Design Build. A separate division that handled full-property design, inside and outside together, so we could be in front of the budget conversation early. The vision was that homeowners could see the whole picture before they spent themselves out of the back yard.

It was the right instinct. I just didn't have time to execute it.

The macro market shifted on us before the pivot took hold. We lost a million dollars worth of work in five weeks. The area we'd grown into couldn't sustain what we'd built at the size we'd built it. I closed the company.

The first lesson out of that wasn't "niche is bad." Our niche was excellent. Our reputation was excellent. Our work was excellent. The lesson was that the second your market starts shifting, every month you wait to widen your lens is a month you're burning runway you didn't know you needed. By the time it's obvious you need to flex, you've usually already lost the time required to flex.

The second lesson was about what actually carried us through the leaner stretches before we closed. It wasn't the new portfolio pieces. It wasn't the SEO. It wasn't the ads we were running. It was the relationships. Past customers calling for the next phase. Neighbors of past customers. The HOA that hired us because the woman three doors down said we'd done a good job for her two years earlier. The relationship economy was the only thing that didn't go quiet when the rest of the market did.

If I were running that company today, with the markets the way they are now, I'd take a wider lane earlier. Not because niche is wrong. Because every customer relationship is an asset that compounds, and every "wrong-shaped" lead I turned down was an asset I declined to start.

// 05 · The marketing efficiency math

Compare the cost to acquire a new customer to the cost of a small job done well.

Pull this comparison if you've never done it.

The cost to acquire a brand-new customer through paid channels right now sits between two hundred and seven hundred dollars per booked lead, depending on the channel and the market. Per Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies, remodel spending is projected to hit $522 billion in 2026, but homeowners are shrinking jobs and shopping harder. A booked lead in 2026 is more expensive and slower to close than the same lead two years ago.

The cost to acquire a referral from a happy past customer is roughly zero. They tell their neighbor about you. The neighbor calls. You skip the entire top of the funnel. Same booked lead, dramatically lower acquisition cost.

A small job done well is the lowest-cost way to manufacture future referrals in the trades. You're not just doing the work. You're paying for marketing in the form of doing work, getting paid for the work, and turning the customer who paid you into your next salesperson.

Every contractor who is dialing in their SEO, running ads, paying for LSAs, and simultaneously turning down "wrong-shaped" leads is leaking marketing dollars on both ends. They're paying to acquire customers through the front door and refusing free customers through the side door.

// 06 · Multiple lenses

Business gets harder when you only look through one lens.

The pure-niche lens says protect the brand, stay focused, don't dilute. The pure-revenue lens says take everything that pays. Both are right sometimes.

The lens I think most owners underuse is the marketing lens. Every job is part of how you get the next job, regardless of size. A small job is a billboard you got paid to install. A medium job is a relationship you got paid to start. A big job is a portfolio piece you got paid to deliver.

Through that lens, the leads outside your usual lane stop being a problem to filter. They become a decision to evaluate. What is this worth in the long run if I treat it like a relationship and not just a job?

That's not the same as saying yes to everything. It's saying you owe the lead a real look before you write it off. Some are still no's. The ones that are yes's are the marketing wins your competitors aren't seeing.

// The closer

Niche is a luxury when the market gives it to you. Nimble is a necessity when it doesn't.

The cheapest marketing dollar in the trades has always been the relationship you build with the customer in front of you. That dollar gets spent every time you take a job and treat it like a long conversation. It gets wasted every time you say no to a "wrong-shaped" lead because you're waiting for a better one.

Markets shift. Seasons change. I closed a hardscape business once because I waited too long to read the season I was in. The relationships we'd already built were the only thing that kept us going as long as it did. The relationships we hadn't built yet, because we'd been too narrow, were the ones we needed and didn't have.

If your phone has been quieter than it used to be, look at the leads you've been turning down lately and ask whether you've been protecting a niche, or whether you've been protecting a habit. There's a real difference. The first one is strategy. The second one is the thing that closes businesses.

The Marketing Pillar

Contractor marketing: the real playbook for trade businesses under $5M.

Which channels actually work, what to budget by revenue stage, when to hire an agency versus doing it yourself, and the mistakes that show up on every contractor's account. Built for trades, not e-commerce.

BY NICK CONLEY FOUNDER · BLUE COLLAR TECHY
// Why most contractor marketing advice is aimed at the wrong business

The marketing advice available to contractors is enormous in volume and almost entirely written for the wrong audience. Most of it assumes a marketing team, a content budget, a dedicated ad manager, and a sales funnel that looks like a SaaS company's. None of that applies to a hardscape crew in Ohio or a plumbing shop in Phoenix.

I ran a $3M hardscape company in Northern California for ten years. I've since spent years inside contractor marketing accounts at Tekton Growth. The thing I keep seeing is that the contractors burning money on marketing are almost always following advice that would work great for a retail brand or a home services franchise. It does not work for an owner-operator trade business with two trucks and seasonal cash flow.

This post is the playbook I actually use. It is organized around the decisions you have to make in the right order. Channels come before budget. Budget comes before the agency decision. Get those reversed and you spend money before you know what you're buying.

Already paying an agency? Read every section. The channels chapter tells you what a competent engagement covers. The mistakes chapter tells you what a bad one looks like. If you can't tell which one you have after reading both, that's already an answer.

// The five channels that actually move the needle

Not every channel works for every contractor. These five do.

The marketing landscape for contractors is not as complicated as it gets sold. There are five channels that consistently deliver leads for trade businesses under $5M. The other dozen things people pitch you are either premature, wrong-fit, or overpriced for what they return at your revenue stage.

1. Local SEO and Google Business Profile

This is the highest-return channel for most contractors who stick with it. When someone searches "paver contractor near me" or "HVAC repair Austin" from their phone, the first thing they see is the map pack. Three listings, a map, and a button to call. If you're not in the three-pack for your core service and market, you don't exist for that searcher.

Local SEO delivers 400 to 450% ROI for construction and home service businesses. That is the highest return of any marketing channel measured across the industry. It is also the slowest to build. You're not going to see map pack movement in two weeks. You'll see it at 60 to 90 days of consistent execution. That timeline is why most contractors abandon it before it pays off.

The full local SEO playbook is in a separate post. The short version: your Google Business Profile has to be fully optimized and actively maintained. Your website needs one real page for each major service. Your name, address, and phone number have to match across every directory. And you have to be consistently collecting reviews. Those four things, done well and kept up, compound into map pack presence over time.

2. Google Local Services Ads

LSAs are the fastest way to get phone calls while organic rankings are building. You pay per lead, not per click. Google verifies your license, insurance, and background check before running the ads. The Google Guarantee badge shows up on your listing. For a new market or a contractor starting from scratch digitally, LSAs are often the first paid channel worth turning on.

The tradeoff is that LSAs are competitive in metro markets and you're bidding against every contractor in your category. Cost per lead varies heavily by trade and market. In construction, Google search ads averaged $165 per lead in 2025. LSAs tend to run lower for established, verified accounts. Dispute bad leads quickly. Google gives you a credit process, and most contractors don't use it.

3. Referral and review systems

Every contractor tells me referrals are their best source of leads. They're usually right. What they're almost never doing is running a system around it. They're waiting for referrals instead of creating the conditions that produce them.

82% of small businesses say referrals are their primary source of new customers. Word-of-mouth converts at two to three times the rate of paid advertising. That is not an argument for ignoring digital. It is an argument for treating referrals as a channel with a real process behind it, not a lucky outcome you hope happens after every job.

The system is simple. Three ask touchpoints after every job. In-person when the crew packs up. A text that night with your Google review link. A follow-up email three days later if no review has appeared. That process, run on every closed job without exception, compounds into both review count and referral volume over 60 to 90 days. The reviews are public and searchable. The referrals are private and warm. Both matter.

4. Google Ads

Google Ads work for contractors at the right stage. That stage is not "I need leads right now and my bank account is low." It is "I have solid organic presence and I want to amplify the demand I'm already capturing." Running Google Ads on top of a working organic base returns significantly more than running them cold on a site with no GBP, no reviews, and no service pages.

The contractors I see burn money on Google Ads are almost always running broad match keywords with no negative list, sending traffic to a homepage instead of a service page, and checking the campaign once a month. That is not a Google Ads problem. That is a management problem. If you're not running Google Ads yourself, whoever manages them needs to be looking at search term reports weekly and cutting waste continuously. That's the job.

5. Email to your existing customer base

This is the most underused channel in contractor marketing. You have a list of past customers. Each one trusted you enough to hire you once. Most of them would hire you again or refer you to someone if you stayed visible to them. Most contractors send nothing after the job is complete. Radio silence for two years, then wonder why repeat business is low.

A simple quarterly email to your past customer list generates real return. Seasonal maintenance reminders. Project spotlights. A referral ask with a photo from a recent job. Nothing complicated. The channel cost is nearly zero. The return on a single repeat job or referral from a former customer pays for the effort a hundred times over. Build the list. Send to it. Do it consistently.

// Budget benchmarks by revenue stage

How much to spend. In contractor terms, not MBA terms.

The standard advice is to spend 5 to 15 percent of revenue on marketing. That range is wide enough to be almost useless. Here is what the breakdown actually looks like by stage.

2026 Marketing Budget Benchmarks — Trade Businesses

What each stage should actually be spending

  • Under $500K revenue: 10 to 15% of revenue. At this stage, you're still building your base. Referrals are your primary channel and they should stay that way, but you need a real GBP, a real website, and a consistent review ask process running before you spend a dollar on paid ads. Focus the budget on organic first.
  • $500K to $1.5M revenue: 8 to 12% of revenue. This is where most contractors add their first paid channel. LSAs or Google Ads on top of a solid organic foundation. The key word is on top. Don't start paid until organic is working. At $1M revenue, 10% is $100K per year or about $8,300 a month. That's enough for a real agency engagement plus a meaningful ad budget.
  • $1.5M to $5M revenue: 5 to 10% of revenue. You have the base built and you're in growth mode or market-hold mode. Growth mode pushes toward 10% and adds channels. Hold mode is closer to 5 to 6% and maintains what's working without adding complexity. The mistake at this stage is cutting marketing too aggressively during a good quarter and losing ground to competitors who kept going.
  • First 3 to 5 years, unfamiliar market: 12 to 20% of revenue. Building a presence from nothing is expensive. There's no existing referral base to compound. Plan for higher spend and a longer timeline to positive return. Most contractors who enter a new market underestimate both.

US residential builders with strong, established referral bases average 2.8 to 3.2% of revenue on marketing. That's the floor for a mature business in a stable market. If you're below that and your pipeline is uncertain, you're underspending for the phase you're in.

The mistake most contractors make is not the dollar amount. It's the sequence. They spend money before the foundation is solid and wonder why the return is low. A $3,000 per month Google Ads campaign sending traffic to a site with no service pages, a three-star GBP, and no review recency is not a marketing investment. It is a drain.

Check the demand picture before you commit to a spend level. If search volume for your primary service is declining in your specific market, that changes the math on how aggressively to invest in SEO versus other channels. // The sequence — build it in this order

Sequence matters more than budget. This is the right order.

Most contractor marketing conversations start with "which channel should I add?" That is the wrong starting question. The right question is "what's the first thing I haven't built yet?" Because the channels stack. Each one works better when the ones below it are solid.

  • Foundation first — organic and reputation. GBP fully built and verified by you. Every major service has its own page on your website. NAP consistent across every directory. Review ask process running after every job. This is the slab. Nothing holds without it.
  • Referral system second — make the referrals intentional. Three-ask touchpoint process in place. Google review link texted to every customer the day of completion. Crew trained on the in-person ask. Past customer email list started. This costs almost nothing and pays continuously.
  • LSAs third — first paid channel for most contractors. Once organic is building and reviews are accumulating, turn on LSAs. Pay per lead. Keep the budget modest and dispute every bad lead. This is the bridge channel while organic SEO compounds.
  • Google Ads fourth — amplify what's already working. When your GBP is ranking in the map pack and your organic site is getting traffic, Google Ads push more volume into a system that's already converting. Starting ads before organic works means paying to drive traffic to a site that can't close the visit.
  • Email fifth — monetize the base you've already built. Your past customer list is an asset most contractors ignore. Quarterly touchpoints keep you visible. One repeat job or referred job per quarter from your list pays for every email you'll ever send.

That sequence is not arbitrary. It reflects where the compounding actually happens. Organic SEO and reputation compound over months. Referrals compound over years. Paid ads don't compound. They run when you're paying and stop when you're not. Build the compounding channels first. Use paid to accelerate.

// Agency vs DIY vs hire-internal — the real decision

Three paths. Each fits a different stage. Here's how to tell which one you're in.

The agency-versus-DIY question comes up constantly in contractor marketing circles. It is almost always asked too early, before the person asking has defined what they need done. The decision depends on your revenue, your time, and your capacity to manage external partners. It is not a values question about self-reliance versus outsourcing. It is a math question.

DIY: best under $500K, before paid channels are running

A basic DIY marketing stack runs $150 to $300 per month. You're handling your own GBP, your own review asks, your own website updates. At this stage, the time investment is the cost. Most owner-operators under $500K are the right person to manage these channels themselves, because the learning curve is real and what you learn matters when you're deciding whether an agency is doing the right things later.

The risk with DIY is that it stays there too long. Contractors who are still fully DIY at $1.5M revenue are almost always leaving something on the table. Not because agencies are magic, but because at that revenue level, the owner's time is worth more than it costs to hire competent help.

Agency: best from $750K to $3M when you're ready to grow

A mid-tier full-service marketing agency runs $3,000 to $6,000 per month on retainer, plus ad spend. A year with a capable agency is $36K to $72K before you count what you're putting into ads. That is a real number. At $2M revenue, that's 2 to 4% of revenue, which fits within normal benchmarks. At $500K, that's 7 to 14% of revenue on the agency alone before ad spend, which puts you outside the budget math entirely.

The agency decision is worth making when two things are true. Your revenue can carry the retainer without squeezing operating cash. And you're ready to invest time managing the relationship. The contractors who get the worst returns from agencies are the ones who hire and disappear. A good agency still needs you to answer questions, approve content, flag when a campaign is sending wrong-quality leads, and review reports. Plan for four to six hours a month minimum. If you don't have that bandwidth, the agency is flying blind half the time.

One thing that differentiates a competent agency from a mediocre one: they should be able to tell you your cost per lead by channel, your map pack ranking positions for your top keywords, and your review recency before you ask. If you have to pull those numbers yourself, you're paying for an agency that isn't actually managing your account.

In-house hire: best above $2M when marketing is complex enough to keep someone busy full-time

A marketing hire at $2M to $3M revenue starts to make sense when the scope is wide enough to keep a person genuinely busy. At $2M, you're managing Google Ads, LSAs, organic SEO, a social presence, email, and potentially video content. That's a full job. It's not a full job at $800K.

The in-house hire needs a clear mandate and real tools. They need access to the GBP, the ad accounts, the CRM data, and the analytics. A marketing hire who can't see the numbers can't do the job. The other thing they need is accountability. A marketing hire with no KPIs and no reporting cadence is expensive overhead. A marketing hire with weekly call volume targets and monthly reporting is an investment.

The decision at a glance

Agency vs DIY vs in-house by stage

  • Under $500K: DIY the fundamentals. GBP, reviews, basic website. Spend $150-$300/month on tools, not retainers.
  • $500K to $1.5M: Consider an agency for local SEO and ad management when you're ready to grow and can carry the retainer. Keep LSAs running yourself until you can verify the agency manages them correctly.
  • $1.5M to $3M: Full-service agency relationship or a capable in-house hire. The math starts to favor in-house above $2.5M if you have complex enough scope to justify it.
  • Above $3M: In-house coordinator plus agency specialists, or a full in-house team. The coordination cost of a single agency managing everything gets high at this revenue level. Specialists outperform generalists.
Referrals compound. Organic SEO compounds. Paid ads run when you're paying and stop when you're not. Build the compounding channels first.

// What doesn't work for trade businesses under $5M

Stop spending here first.

The marketing tactics being sold to contractors include a lot of things that work for other businesses and don't work for you. Knowing what to stop is as important as knowing what to start.

Social media as a lead generation channel

Instagram and Facebook posts do not generate contractor leads at meaningful volume. They build brand familiarity over long time horizons with people who already follow you. The contractor who posts every day and gets no calls is not a failure at social media. They're using the wrong tool for the job they're trying to do.

Social works for contractors in exactly two scenarios. Before-and-after content that gets shared by customers into neighbor groups and community feeds. And targeted Facebook and Instagram ads to homeowners in a specific zip code radius with a specific creative. That second one is paid social, not organic social, and it works best for longer-consideration jobs like outdoor living, kitchen remodels, and home additions, not emergency service calls.

If you're spending more than an hour a week on organic social posts and not running paid social, redirect that time to your review ask process or your email list. The return is not comparable.

Angi, HomeAdvisor, and lead aggregators at scale

Lead aggregators sell the same lead to five contractors. You're competing on price the moment the call comes in. The homeowner has already talked to two other contractors before you pick up. The close rate is low. The cost per closed job is high. And the lead quality is inconsistent in ways you can't control.

These services are not worthless. For a new contractor with no organic presence and no referral base, they fill a gap. The mistake is staying dependent on them once organic channels are viable. The contractors who run Google Business Profile well, collect reviews consistently, and have a working website eventually stop needing aggregators. That's the goal. Use them to bridge, not to build the long-term base.

SEO packages priced as a one-time setup

One-time SEO engagements are everywhere in contractor marketing. Someone charges $1,500 to $3,000 to "set up your SEO." They claim your GBP, add some keywords to your homepage, and submit to some directories. Then they're done.

That is the foundation. Not the building. Map pack rankings require ongoing signals. Review recency builds or decays. Photo freshness matters. Competitors don't stop moving. An SEO setup gives you a starting point. What holds the position is continued execution. If someone is selling you a one-time SEO fix with no ongoing component, they're selling you the first floor and leaving before the house is framed.

High-production video content before other fundamentals are in place

Video is a real tool for contractor marketing. YouTube specifically is underused and worth building toward. But I regularly talk to contractors who are spending $800 a month on video production for social media while their GBP has 11 reviews and their website loads in eight seconds on mobile.

Video works as an amplifier. It amplifies brand trust for people already considering you. It amplifies review ask results when you send a short crew video with the link. It amplifies referrals when a finished project video gets shared in a neighborhood group. It does not replace the fundamentals. Build the base first. Then video becomes a powerful layer on top of it.

// The mistakes I keep seeing on contractor accounts

These come up on almost every account I look at.

After reviewing enough contractor marketing accounts, the same patterns appear. Not industry-specific or market-specific. They show up in Atlanta and Phoenix and Cleveland. Here's the list.

No attribution tracking

The most common mistake in contractor marketing is not knowing where leads come from. The owner is paying for Google Ads, LSAs, a lead service, and an SEO retainer simultaneously and has no real data on which one is producing the closed jobs. They guess. Usually they guess wrong.

You do not need sophisticated software to fix this. A simple intake process that asks every new caller "how did you find us?" and records the answer gives you the data you need to cut what isn't working and put more money into what is. Contractors using CRM and analytics platforms achieve 15 to 20% higher marketing ROI on average. That gap is almost entirely explained by attribution. Know what's working before you spend more of it.

Spreading budget thin across too many channels

A $4,000 monthly marketing budget spread across Google Ads, Facebook Ads, LSAs, a lead service, and an SEO retainer is five weak campaigns instead of two strong ones. Thin budgets on paid channels mean the algorithms don't have enough data to optimize. Thin budgets on organic channels mean the execution is rushed and shallow. Pick fewer channels and fund them properly. Win one channel before adding the next.

Running paid ads before organic is working

Google Ads traffic lands somewhere. If it lands on a slow website with one generic services page, a two-star GBP, and no reviews, the cost per lead from ads goes up and the close rate goes down. The organic and reputation foundation does not just affect organic rankings. It affects how well paid traffic converts. Fix the foundation first. Then pay to drive more traffic into a system that can handle it.

Letting reviews go stale

A contractor with 60 reviews, the most recent from seven months ago, is losing ground to a competitor with 35 reviews and three from last week. Google weights recency. Searchers weight recency. "11 months ago" on a review does not inspire confidence in a homeowner trying to decide who shows up next Tuesday.

The ask process has to run on every job, every time. It cannot be a campaign that runs once and stops. Assign the in-person ask to the crew lead. Assign the text follow-up to whoever handles job completion paperwork. Automate the email follow-up if you can. Make it impossible to close a job without the ask happening. Review recency is a direct ranking factor and a direct trust signal. Treat it accordingly.

Changing direction before the current strategy has time to work

Local SEO takes 90 to 120 days of consistent execution before meaningful ranking movement shows up. Google Ads optimization takes four to six weeks of data before the algorithm learns enough to improve performance. Email to a past customer list takes multiple sends before it becomes a reliable source of repeat jobs.

The contractors who get the worst return on marketing are almost never the ones with the wrong strategy. They're the ones who execute for six weeks, see no dramatic results, and switch to a new approach. Rinse, repeat, wonder why nothing works. Pick the right channels for your stage. Execute for at least 90 days without changing direction. Review what the data actually says. Then adjust.

// Where to start

The prioritized action list. Work down from the first gap you find.

If you read everything above and are trying to figure out where your biggest gap is, use this list. The first item you can't honestly check off is where you start. Everything below it can wait.

  • GBP claimed by you, fully built, actively maintained. If an agency has owner access and you've parted ways, reclaim it. Give active vendors manager access only. Profile completeness, photo freshness, and post frequency are all ongoing tasks. Not a setup and forget.
  • Review ask process runs after every job without exception. In-person at completion, text the same day, email follow-up at 72 hours. Every crew, every job. No exceptions. If this isn't happening consistently, every other marketing activity is running uphill.
  • One real page per major service on your website. Not a bullet on an overview page. A page with a title tag, a service description, your market area, and a clear call to action. If someone searches that service in your city and you have no page for it, you have zero chance of ranking for it.
  • NAP matches across every directory you can see. Google, Yelp, Angi, BBB, Houzz, Facebook, Apple Maps. Same business name, same format, same phone number. Every mismatch erodes your local authority. Run through the major ones and clean what's wrong.
  • Mobile page speed is above 50 on PageSpeed Insights. Enter your URL at the free Google tool. Below 50 means your site's technical performance is suppressing rankings before content, category, or review signals even apply. Fix this before you add any paid channels.
  • You know where your leads actually come from. Ask every new caller how they found you. Record the answer. Review it monthly. If you've been paying for marketing for more than six months and can't answer this question from real data, stop adding channels until you can.
  • Your past customer list gets a quarterly email. Even a simple note with a project photo and a referral ask. This channel costs almost nothing and the return per closed referral or repeat job is significant. If you don't have a list, start building one today from every job you've completed this year.
  • Paid channels are running on top of working organic, not in place of it. Google Ads and LSAs belong above a foundation that's producing. If organic isn't working yet, fix it before spending on paid. The paid traffic has nowhere productive to go until the site and reputation support it.

That list is the whole game for a trade business under $5M. You do not need 12 channels. You need three or four channels executed properly and consistently. The contractors I've watched grow from $800K to $3M did it with a strong GBP, a working website, a real review process, and eventually a managed ad account on top. No content team. No influencer strategy. No complicated funnel. Just the fundamentals, done well, maintained over time.

Before you commit to a spend level or a channel mix, check what search demand looks like in your specific market. A contractor in a growing market can invest aggressively in SEO and paid search and ride the tide. A contractor in a flat or contracting market needs a different allocation.

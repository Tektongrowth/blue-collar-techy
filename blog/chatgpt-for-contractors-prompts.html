AI for Contractors 2026

ChatGPT for contractors: 15 prompts that save you real time.

ChatGPT will not run your business. It will write your scopes, follow-ups, and review replies faster than you can at 9 p.m. Here are 15 prompts to copy, the privacy setting to change first, and the review rules you cannot ignore.

BY NICK CONLEY FOUNDER · BLUE COLLAR TECHY
// Why most contractors get bad answers

Treat ChatGPT like a new office hire.

ChatGPT writes fast and knows nothing about your business. It does not know your prices, your crew, your service area, or what you promised the customer on the phone. Give it those things and it saves you real time. Leave them out and you get generic filler.

I ran a hardscape company for ten years. The paperwork was always what ate my evenings: scopes, follow-ups, change orders, review replies. That is the work ChatGPT is good at. It is a writing tool. It is not an estimator, a lawyer, or a source of facts.

Below are 15 prompts you can copy and fill in. Anything in [brackets] is yours to replace. Three rules first, because they matter more than any prompt.

  1. You supply every number. Prices, dimensions, dates, warranty terms. ChatGPT will happily invent them.
  2. You read every word before it goes out. You are the one signing the proposal.
  3. You decide what customer information goes in. More on that next.
// Before you paste anything

Set up your privacy settings first.

By default, OpenAI may use the content of your chats to improve its models for consumer accounts. You can turn that off. In ChatGPT, open your profile, go to Settings, then Data Controls, and switch off "Improve the model for everyone." Your chat history stays, and new conversations are not used for training. OpenAI explains this in its help center (linked in the sources below), and the wording of the settings changes from time to time, so check it yourself.

Then build a habit. Leave out the customer's last name, phone number, email, and street address. Write "the homeowner" and "the property on the north side of town." You lose nothing, because the draft does not need those details. You add the real ones when you paste the finished text into your email or proposal.

Do the same with your pricing sheet and your margins. If you would not hand it to a competitor, do not paste it in.

// Estimates and proposals

Prompts for the paperwork that eats your evenings.

These work best when you give ChatGPT your rough notes and let it organize them. Your notes are the accurate part. The prompt just makes them readable.

Prompt 1. Scope of work from site notes
I run a [type of contractor] company in [city or region]. Turn these site notes into a clear scope of work a homeowner can understand. Use short sections: what we will do, what is included, what is not included, and what could change the price. Do not add any work, materials, or prices I did not mention. If something seems missing, list it as a question for me at the end. Notes: [paste your rough notes, no customer name or address]
Prompt 2. Plain-English explanation of a price
Here is the price breakdown for a [project type]: [paste line items and totals]. Write a short, friendly paragraph I can put in the proposal that explains why the price is what it is. Do not use jargon. Do not change any numbers. Do not promise a result or a date.
Prompt 3. Change order
Write a change order for a job already in progress. Original scope: [short summary]. The change: [what changed and why]. Added cost: $[amount]. Added time: [days]. Keep it polite and factual. Include a line asking the customer to approve in writing before we start the extra work.
Prompt 4. Follow-up on an unanswered quote
Write three short follow-up messages for a customer who got a quote from me [number] days ago and has not replied. Message 1 is a friendly check-in by text. Message 2 is an email that offers to answer questions. Message 3 is a polite last note. No pressure, no fake deadlines, no discounts. Under 60 words each.

On prompt 4: fast follow-up matters more than clever wording. I get into the data on that in the post on missed calls and speed to lead.

// Customer communication

Prompts for the messages you dread writing.

Most bad customer relationships do not start with bad work. They start with silence. A schedule slips, nobody says anything, and the customer fills the gap with the worst assumption. These prompts help you say the thing early.

Prompt 5. Delay notice
Write a short text and a longer email telling a customer their [project type] start date is moving from [old date] to [new date] because of [real reason]. Own the delay, give the new date, say what happens next, and do not blame the customer, the weather, or suppliers more than the facts support. Calm and respectful.
Prompt 6. Reply to a frustrated customer
A customer sent me this message: [paste it, with names removed]. Draft a reply that acknowledges what they are upset about, states what I can do and by when, and asks for a short call if that would help. Do not admit fault for anything I have not told you was my fault. Do not be defensive. Give me two versions, one shorter than the other.
Prompt 7. Scheduling text
Write a text confirming tomorrow's arrival window for the crew at the customer's home. Include the window, what the crew will do first, and anything the customer needs to move or clear. Friendly, one short paragraph.
Prompt 8. Project wrap-up email
Write a wrap-up email for a finished [project type]. Thank the customer, explain how to care for the work in [number] short bullet points, state the warranty exactly as I give it here: [paste your warranty terms], and ask whether they are happy with the result. Do not ask for a review yet.
AI writes the first draft. You sign the proposal.
A rule worth keeping
// Reviews and your Google profile

Prompts for reviews, with the guardrails.

Reviews drive who gets the call. They are also where contractors can get in trouble, so read this section twice.

The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials has been in effect since October 21, 2024. It bans fake reviews, buying reviews that express a chosen sentiment, and review suppression through intimidation or groundless legal threats. Knowing violations can bring civil penalties, currently up to $53,088 per violation. The FTC's own guidance says a business can send a general request for reviews and can offer incentives, as long as it does not require or imply that the review must be positive. Do not use ChatGPT to write a review and post it under a customer's name or your own. That is the fake review the rule is aimed at. This is not legal advice. Read the FTC's Q&A, and ask a lawyer if you are unsure.

Prompt 9. Review request text
Write a short text asking a customer to leave an honest Google review of our [type of work] job. Thank them first. Say that honest feedback of any kind helps us. Do not offer anything in exchange, and do not suggest what the review should say. Under 50 words. Leave a spot for the link: [review link].
Prompt 10. Reply to a positive review
Write three different short replies to this Google review: [paste review]. Use the customer's first name only. Mention one specific thing from the review. Avoid stock phrases like 'we appreciate your business.' Each under 40 words.
Prompt 11. Reply to a negative review
Draft a public reply to this negative Google review: [paste review]. Here is what actually happened, in my words: [your honest account]. Keep it calm and factual. Do not accuse the reviewer of lying, do not share private job details, do not make threats, and invite them to call me at [number] to fix it. Under 100 words.

For prompt 11, never post the first draft in a hurry. Wait an hour. Read it out loud. If it sounds like you, post it.

// Marketing

Prompts for your website and profile, used carefully.

This is where AI does the most damage when used lazily. A page of generic text about "quality craftsmanship" does nothing for you. Google and customers can both tell. The fix is to feed it specifics that only you have: the neighborhoods you work in, the problems you solve, the way you run a job.

Prompt 12. Service page outline
I am a [type of contractor] in [city]. Outline a service page for [service]. Suggest headings and the questions a homeowner usually asks before hiring for this. Do not write the page. I will fill in the details from my own jobs. Do not make up statistics.
Prompt 13. Google Business Profile post
Write a short Google Business Profile post about a recent [project type] we finished in [neighborhood or city]. Details: [what we did, how long it took, anything unusual]. Plain language, one sentence on what the homeowner wanted, a clear call to action to call [number]. Under 120 words.
Prompt 14. Photo captions
Here are notes on 5 job photos: [list what each shows]. Write a short, factual caption for each that a homeowner could understand. Mention the material or technique where I gave it. Do not exaggerate.
Prompt 15. Check my own draft
Here is a draft of my [service page or post]: [paste]. Find places where it sounds generic, claims something without proof, or uses words a homeowner would not use. List what to fix. Do not rewrite it.

Prompt 15 is my favorite. Having ChatGPT critique your writing works better than having it write for you. I wrote about what happens when you let an AI tool publish for you unchecked in my test of a $99 AI SEO tool. It invented a case study. Check everything.

// What to do next

Pick three prompts and use them this week.

Do not try all 15. Pick the three that match the writing you already put off. For most contractors that is the scope of work, the quote follow-up, and the review reply. Run each one on a real job this week. Fix the prompt until the output sounds like you. Save the version that works in a notes file.

After a month you will have a small library of prompts that fit your business. That library is worth more than any list I can give you.

AI tools for contractors: what's worth using, what's a waste.

Personal picks. Tools I actually use at Tekton Growth for voice notes, proposals, review responses, and follow-up drafting. The use cases that have real payoff. And the stuff that looks good in a demo but doesn't survive a crew.

BY NICK CONLEY FOUNDER · BLUE COLLAR TECHY
// The noise is real

AI for contractors has been the pitch of the year. Every software company has an "AI feature." Every marketing agency is selling an "AI strategy." Every tool newsletter has a list of 40 things a contractor could theoretically use.

Most of that content was written by people who have never bid a job, managed a crew, or stared at an unfinished proposal at 9pm after a full day on site.

I run Tekton Growth. We work with contractors day to day on marketing, local SEO, and systems. I also ran a hardscape company in Northern California for ten years before that. AI tools are part of my actual workflow now, in specific ways, because they solve real problems I recognize from both sides.

This is not a ranked list of 30 tools. This is what I actually use, what I recommend to the contractors we work with, and what I tell people to skip. The goal is not to add subscriptions. It's to save time on the specific things that eat time in a contracting business.

If you want a 47-item AI roundup, there are a hundred of them. This one is shorter and more honest.

// What AI is actually good for

Start with the problem, not the tool.

Before the tools, start with the use cases. A tool only matters if there's a real problem underneath it.

AI writing tools are good at two things most contractors genuinely need. Getting words out faster. And turning rough notes into something readable.

Getting words out faster matters when you're writing a proposal at 9pm. It matters when you're responding to a three-star Google review and you can't figure out how to sound like a real person instead of a customer-service department. It matters when you're writing Google Ads headlines and you've written the same sentence six times and none of them feel right.

Turning rough notes into something readable matters when you've walked a customer through a $40,000 project and talked for 45 minutes. You know every detail. You explained the drainage plan in exactly the right terms. But by the time you're back at your desk, what's left is fragments. AI tools can take a voice recording of that conversation and turn it into text you can actually use in a proposal.

Both of those are real, daily problems in a contracting business. AI solves both of them well, for under $30 a month. Everything else is mostly noise.

// The tools that work

Two tools. Four use cases.

Tool 01 Voice recording · $8/month

Otter.ai: turn the job walk into a proposal starting point.

The problem this solves is specific. Contractors are talkers, not typists. You know the job. You walked it. You explained the drainage issue to the homeowner in exactly the right terms standing in their backyard. Getting those terms from your head to a proposal document is friction, and friction is where the details get lost.

Otter.ai records your conversation, transcribes it in real time, and delivers a searchable summary with the key discussion points pulled out. You walk the job site with your phone. You talk through the scope with the customer out loud, same as you always do. By the time you're back in the truck, you have a text document ready to paste into your proposal workflow.

No typing on a small screen while standing on someone's patio. No trying to reconstruct a conversation from three jobs ago. No calling the customer back to confirm something you already said you understood.

The workflow

Open Otter at the start of the walkthrough. Talk normally. At the end, you have a transcript. Paste it into your AI writing tool. Ask it to summarize the scope and customer concerns in bullet form. That list is your proposal foundation.

Price: $8.33 a month on the annual plan. Honest caveat: audio quality matters. On a loud job site with equipment running, you'll get some garbled sections. Use it for the client conversation before the crew starts, not mid-pour.

Tool 02 AI writing · $20/month

ChatGPT Plus or Claude Pro: proposals, review responses, follow-up, ads.

Contractors who try AI writing tools tend to bounce between four different ones and never get good at any. The ones who get the most out of it pick one and learn to use it properly.

My take: ChatGPT Plus or Claude Pro. Both are $20 a month. Both have free tiers that work for light use. If you're sending more than a couple of proposals a week, or responding to reviews regularly, the paid version is better and the time savings cover the cost in the first week. Pick one and stay with it for at least 60 days before deciding it doesn't work for you.

Here is what each use case actually looks like in practice.

Proposals.

You have the job scope in your head, or in an Otter transcript. Open the writing tool and give it context.

Sample prompt I'm a landscape contractor. I just did a site walk for a 1,400 sq ft paver patio with a retaining wall, drainage system, and lighting prep. The customer is most concerned about runoff from the slope on the north side. They want to finish before the end of September. Write a clear scope of work paragraph I can include in my proposal.

It writes a first draft. You edit it to match your actual numbers and the way you talk. You spend 10 minutes instead of 45. The proposal is better because the language is cleaner, not because you let a machine write the whole thing.

Review responses.

You got a three-star review. The customer wasn't happy with how cleanup was handled at the end. You want to respond professionally without sounding defensive or corporate.

Sample prompt Here is a negative Google review I need to respond to as the owner of a landscaping company: [paste review text]. Write a response that acknowledges the concern, briefly explains what happened, and invites them to reach out directly. Sound like a real person, not a PR statement.

Edit the output before posting. Read it out loud. Cut anything that doesn't sound like how you'd actually say it. Three minutes total and the response is better than most contractors manage.

Follow-up messages.

You quoted a $22,000 outdoor living project three weeks ago. You haven't heard back. You need to follow up without sounding like you need the job.

Sample prompt Write a short, professional follow-up text to a homeowner I quoted a patio and pergola project for three weeks ago. I want to check in and see if they have questions, not push for a decision. Direct and relaxed tone, not salesy.

Edit it so it sounds like how you actually text people. Send it from your own number. Customers respond to this more often than a form email because it feels personal.

Google Ads copy.

You need five headline options and two description lines for a new campaign. You don't want to stare at a blank text field for 20 minutes.

Sample prompt Write five Google Ads headlines for a hardscape contractor in Sacramento serving homeowners who want patio and outdoor living builds. Selling points: 15-year craftsmanship, licensed and insured, no-deposit payment terms. Each headline must be under 30 characters.

You get eight or ten options. Three of them are usable. You pick the best ones and test them. Total time: about four minutes.

The rule that makes all of it work

Context in, quality out. The more specific you are about your trade, your location, the customer situation, and the format you need, the better the result. A vague prompt gets a vague output. That is not the tool failing. That is the prompt failing.

The rule that protects you

Read every output before it goes anywhere. AI is the first draft, not the final draft. A proposal that sounds like a corporate press release loses you the job. A review response that doesn't match the situation makes you look worse than no response. Read it, cut what doesn't sound like you, then send it.

// What to skip

The tools that aren't worth it.

As important as what to use is what to stop paying for, or what to never start.

  • The $99 AI SEO tools. I bought one and ran it through every feature for a review post on this site. It generated fabricated statistics and a case study for a plumbing company that doesn't exist. No GBP audit, no citation building, no local execution. Just content that looks like local SEO from the outside. Read the full test here.
  • The all-in-one "AI business suite" at $97/month. Thirty features, most of which you will never configure. You'll use three of them. Buy the specific tool that solves the specific problem you have today.
  • Automation tools labeled "AI" that aren't. Scheduling tools, review request pipelines, CRM follow-up sequences. These are good tools. They're not AI tools. The AI label is marketing. Evaluate them on what they actually do, not what the pricing page says they are.
  • Any tool that publishes without your eyes on it. An AI that auto-publishes blog posts or social content while you're on site will eventually publish something wrong. Your name is on it. Review everything before it goes out.
  • Multiple AI chat tools at the same time. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity. Pick one. The marginal difference between them for contractor writing tasks is not worth the cost or the attention split. Get good at prompting one tool and it will do more than four tools used badly.
// The stack and what it costs

Under $30 a month for the whole setup.

If you built the AI stack I'd recommend for most trade businesses right now, here's what it looks like:

Tool What it does Cost
Otter.ai Voice recording, transcription, meeting summary $8/mo
ChatGPT Plus or Claude Pro Proposals, review responses, follow-up, ad copy $20/mo
Total $28/mo

That is less than what most contractors spend on fuel additives. And it is worth more than most of the $97-a-month AI subscriptions being marketed to trade businesses right now.

One check before you buy: look at what your current software already includes. If you're in GoHighLevel, CompanyCam, Jobber, or JobNimbus, there's a real chance AI writing features are already in your plan. Some of them handle proposal drafts and follow-up templates natively. Don't buy a separate tool for something your CRM already does.

Start with one use case. Don't buy two tools and run four workflows on day one. Pick the problem that costs you the most time right now. For most contractors, that's proposals or review responses. Build one habit in the first 30 days. Add a second thing after it's running.

// The closer

You don't need to understand how it works to use it.

Every contractor who's gotten good at estimating learned it by running estimates, not by studying estimating theory. AI tools work the same way. You learn by using them on real jobs, real proposals, and real customer situations.

If you've been telling yourself you'll look into AI when things slow down, that's backwards. Slow periods are when every efficiency advantage matters most. Busy periods are when you're most likely to send a sloppy proposal or miss a follow-up that would have closed.

The tools are cheap. The use cases are specific. Most contractors aren't using them not because of hype or confusion. It's because nobody's shown them what to actually do.

That's what this post was for.

Next step: open one AI tool, describe your business and your trade, and ask it to help you write a follow-up message to a job you quoted in the last 30 days. See what comes back. Edit it. Send it. That's the whole learning curve for the first use case.
